What the page shows

A robot sees only part of a room. To act safely around people and things, it has to know where everything is, including what its camera cannot see. This page shows a Cadence brain doing that: it keeps a map of what is where, and a thing that goes out of sight stays on the map.

The room

The left half of the page is a room seen from above. The right half is the robot’s brain.

  • The robot carries a camera. The lit area is what the camera sees, and in view it reports each thing’s position about a centimetre off.
  • Out of view are the hatched shadows of the pillar and the arm, and everything beside or behind the robot. The camera reports nothing there.
  • The robot’s arm has two links on a base. Its joints carry sensors that read their angles.
  • Three things stand in the room: a hand, an apple and a slice of bread.

You can drag the hand, the apple, the bread and the pillar. Drag the floor to turn the robot, and click the robot to switch its camera off and on. Rest the mouse anywhere in the room and a few words beside it say what a drag and a click do there.

The map

The brain holds a map of what is where. The map is a sheet of 32 by 26 cells laid over the room, each about 6 cm across. Every cell has 16 units, and their pattern names a kind of thing: hand, apple or bread. A thing on the map is a small patch of active cells at its place, carrying the pattern of its kind. So at any moment the map reads like “a hand here, an apple there, bread over there”. Several things can be on it at once, each tied to its kind.

The map starts empty, and nothing about the room is given to the brain beforehand. Only the senses change the map, by these rules:

  1. A thing enters the map the first time a sense reports it.
  2. Seen: a thing the camera sees is written where it is seen.
  3. Out of sight: a thing the camera does not see stays at its last known place. Nothing moves it.
  4. Seen empty: if the camera sees that last place and the thing is gone, it moved while unseen. The map spreads it over the cells out of sight within 15 cm of that place. If there are no such cells, the map keeps the kind with no place.
  5. Sight only takes away: once a thing is spread over a patch, every cell of the patch the camera sees is removed from it. The patch only shrinks.
  6. Found: when the camera reports the thing again, the patch clears and the thing is written where it is seen.

In the room, the teal glow is where the map holds the hand. The other things on the map are marked with pins where the map holds them.

Asking the map. Tap a thing to ask where the map holds it, or tap the floor to ask what is there. The brain answers by settling: its neurons drive each other until their activity rests, and the answer is read from the resting state. A line from the robot shows the answer.

The “On the map” line

The line under the room lists every thing on the map and how the map holds it:

seen
The camera reports it at this moment.
felt
The robot’s joint readings place it at this moment. This is the robot’s own hand.
heard
A sound places it. Sounds come only from loaded scene files.
held · 3 s
Kept at its last known place. The time is how long ago a sense last placed it.
near · 2 s
Its last place was seen empty, and it lies somewhere on the patch of hidden cells close by.
not known where
The map knows the thing by its kind and holds no place for it.

The robot’s hand is on the map in both settings of the switch, because the robot always knows its own hand.

The two hands

The switch above the room picks which hand the page follows.

A person’s hand moves by itself. The brain knows it only through the camera. When you drag it into a shadow, the map holds it at its last known place. Once the camera sees that place empty, the map holds it near where it vanished. The map keeps no path, so for a moving hand it holds the area where the hand can be. The exact point inside that area is unknown.

The robot’s own hand is the end of its arm. The robot reads its joint angles at every moment, the way you know where your hand is with your eyes closed. Those readings place the hand on the map whether the camera sees it or not.

The apple and the bread

The apple and the bread show the map holding several things, each tied to its kind: an apple behind you, a slice of bread in front of you.

  • Turn the robot away from the apple. The apple leaves the view, and the map keeps it behind the robot, at its place. Nobody moved it, so the place is exact.
  • The bread stands behind the robot when the page opens, so it is off the map. Turn the robot toward it: the bread enters the map the moment the camera first sees it.
  • Hide the apple in a shadow while the robot looks away, then turn back. The camera sees the apple’s old place empty, and the map holds it near there, or knows it by its kind with no place.

When the arm moves

The arm moves only when you move it, and it never moves by itself.

  • With The robot’s own hand chosen, tap the floor or drag the hand, and the arm reaches there.
  • A loaded scene file moves the arm as the file records it.
  • With A person’s hand chosen, the arm stands folded and casts its shadow.

The brain

The right half draws every neuron and every synapse of the map: 29,168 neurons and 39,936 synapses, none of them taught. It is laid out as a brain seen from the side, with each region placed where the part whose role it plays sits in a brain.

Every region’s name carries a small question mark. Rest the pointer on a region or its name and its card opens, telling what the region does at this moment. The card stays while the pointer is on the region or on the card, and a click keeps it open. Click a bundle of synapses between two regions to see what it carries. “Follow the position” walks through the nine steps a position takes through the map.

Each moment of the room, 33 ms, the map settles in two sweeps. The time it took shows at the bottom of the brain.

Details

Details in the top bar opens a chart of the hand’s distance from the true hand over the last ten seconds. Three estimates get the same camera reports: the map, the last seen place, and a constant-velocity Kalman filter that carries the hand on along its seen movement. Shaded stretches are out of view. Under the chart are the brain’s time for one moment, the frame time and the number of cells that hold the hand.

Load a scene replays a recorded scene file in the format cadence-tracking-scene/1. The brain then gets exactly what the file holds, and the chart compares it on that scene.

What the map does not do

The map moves nothing it does not sense. For a hidden person’s hand it holds the patch where the hand can be. Predicting where an unseen thing goes, from movements seen before, is learned physics, planned for a later Cadence release.

Things to try

  • Drag the hand behind the pillar, then bring it back.
  • Turn the robot away from the apple, then toward the bread.
  • Hide the apple in a shadow while the robot looks away, then turn back.
  • Tap the robot to switch its camera off, move things, and switch it on again.
  • Move the pillar to make a new shadow.
  • Choose The robot’s own hand and tap a spot in a shadow.
  • Press H to show where hidden things truly are.
  • Press Tutorial for a four-step walk through all of this.
