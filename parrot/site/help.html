Parrot

An African Grey parrot sits on a perch with a few things on the table round it. Ask it a question about one of them, from the list or out loud, and it answers in sound. Beside it, its brain is drawn as a bird’s brain, live.

What it shows about CadenceSounds as symbols. Each sound the parrot keeps is a record in its long-term memory, and synapses link that record to what it means: a thing, an answer word or a question. Hearing reads the links from a sound to its meaning; speaking reads them backwards, from a word to its sound. Between the two, a trained Cadence brain settles on the answer. The brain has real memory and a real state, and runs in real time in your browser.

What you see

The room with the parrot fills the left half of the screen; its brain, live, fills the right half. Its parts:

  1. The question, as a subtitle at the foot of the room. Each word lights as the parrot hears it.
  2. Its answer, in a bubble above its head, said with the sound it keeps for the word. A ? after the word means it guessed; a ? alone means it did not understand.
  3. Its focus: the yellow ring round the thing it attends to, on the table. A question is about the thing in focus.
  4. The brain, on the right, seen from its left side. Each dot is a neuron, lit by its activity; the lines are the tracts between regions. Tags name the word it hears and the word it says. Drag to turn it; point at a region to read what it does.
  5. Voice, the box at the top right of the brain: the word it says, while it says it.
  6. Ears, the box at the bottom left of the brain: the sound it hears, as frequency bands over time, and the word it takes it for.
  7. Retina L and R, the boxes at the top left of the brain: what each eye sees.
  8. Dopamine, beside the Ears box: lights when a pistachio reaches its brain.
  9. The question card, at the bottom right: Listen to me or Curious, the questions, Ask, Pistachio, Mic, a line that says what to try next, and the last exchange.
  10. The brain switch and How this brain works, at the top right, which explains the brain.

Why it matters

A word is a sound that stands for something. For the parrot that link is a memory it keeps and can change: a sound brings back its meaning, and a meaning brings back its sound. Because the link is memory, one paid hearing is enough to learn a new one. Ask a question in your own voice; if it guessed and got it right, a pistachio links your sound to that question, and the next time it understands you.

A brain that keeps symbols this way learns names and words during its life, from the people round it, with no retraining.

What you can do

  • Ask a question from the list. Pick it and press Ask. The thing it is about comes into focus, and the question plays into the parrot’s ear as a spoken sentence.
  • Ask out loud. With Mic on (your browser asks first), click a thing and ask about it: “What colour is this?”, “Can you eat it?”, “Where is the cup?”
  • Give a pistachio after a right answer. The brain takes its answer as a small lesson, and the sound it heard is linked to what it took it for.
  • When it shows a ?, say it again. When it guessed and the answer was right, give it a pistachio.
  • Listen to me. It looks at you. Say a short word: it keeps it and says it back. Say it again and its sound of the word gets cleaner.
  • Talk over it. Speak while it says a word, and it stops.
  • Move things. Click a thing to put it in focus; drag it to move it. Drag empty space to turn the view, and scroll to zoom.
  • Forget all sounds starts again from the sounds it came with. Reloading the page does the same.

How it works

The ear. The microphone’s sound becomes 40 frequency bands every 10 ms, the same arithmetic as the Cadence library’s speech front end. The ear cuts it into utterances, from where a voice starts to the quiet after it, and leaves out what is not voice-like: a fan, a click, a hum, typing.

The sound memory. Each utterance is a record in the library’s long-term memory (cadence.LongTermMemory), keyed by a code of its sound made with cadence.senses.Ear. A new take is lined up in time with the records of a like length, and the nearest records vote on what it means. A sound told again moves its record toward the new take, so the record holds the mean of its takes and gets cleaner. A record heard once fades; one heard often lasts.

The links. Synapses (cadence.SynapticMemory) link each record to its meaning. The parrot starts with one woman’s questions and answer words and thirty-six other voices, each saying every question and answer word once, every record linked to its meaning. A question it knows is followed word by word as it is said.

The trained brain. It hears each word of the question as it is spoken, one word a moment (300 ms), and sees each thing on the table as its training world described it. At its turn it settles: every neuron moves toward agreement with its inputs until the whole brain agrees, and the voice neuron that settles highest is its answer.

The voice. The parrot plays the sound linked to the answer word, in a parrot voice. It hears its own voice come back and learns how loud that is; a voice well above it is someone else’s, and it stops.

The pistachio. One small lesson for the brain and for the links of the sounds used. A guess that no pistachio follows is doubted for a while.

Limits

  • Questions it was taught. It understands the questions of the list, in the wordings its starting memory holds, about the things on the table, and single answer words. It matches a whole utterance to the sounds it keeps and does not take a free sentence apart.
  • New voices. A voice it never heard is understood less often than the voices it starts with (the numbers above). A noisy room makes it worse.
  • One thing at a time. It answers about the thing in focus.
  • Its eyes. The trained brain does not see the drawn room: the page reports each thing to it as its training world did (shape, size, hue, surface, direction and distance). It does not see a duck or a pear.
  • Its voice. The brain picks the answer word. The sound of each word it starts with was rendered ahead of time into a parrot voice; a word you teach it is said as a pitched-up copy of your sound.
  • Learning. The brain was trained ahead of time, outside the browser. In the page a pistachio is one small lesson. Every visit starts from the shipped memory.
  • Wrong answers. It gets some questions wrong, so they are not on the list: among them the material of some things and the colour of the green ball.
  • Where it runs. Chrome on a desktop or laptop. The first load fetches tens of megabytes.
