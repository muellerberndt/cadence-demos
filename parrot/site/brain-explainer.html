How the parrot’s brain works

How the parrot hears a question, what a sound means to it, how it finds its answer, how it says it, and how a pistachio teaches it.

Which brainA trained Cadence brain answers the questions. Beside it, a hearing layer keeps the sounds and their meanings, and a body sees the table, turns the head and keeps the focus. All three run in your browser.

At a glance

A question takes one path through the parrot, and the answer takes the same path back.

  1. Hearing. The ear cuts the sound into an utterance. The sound memory finds the records nearest it, and their links say what it means: a question, or a word.
  2. Answering. The trained brain hears the question’s words, sees the thing in focus, and settles on an answer word.
  3. Speaking. The answer word is read back through the links to the sound kept for it, and the voice says that sound.

Sounds as symbols

A sound is a record. Every utterance the parrot keeps, a word or a whole question, is a record in the Cadence library’s long-term memory. The record holds the sound itself as frequency bands over time; it never holds letters.

A record is linked to its meaning. Synapses link each record to what it stands for: a word such as “apple”, or a question such as “what colour is this”. The parrot starts with records of one woman’s voice and thirty-six other voices, each linked to its meaning.

Hearing a new take. The take is lined up in time with the records of a like length, frame by frame, so a word said faster or slower lines up too. The nearest records vote: where they agree on a meaning, the take is understood. Where they do not, the parrot guesses and shows a ?.

Told again. A take of a sound it knows moves that record toward the take, so a record holds the mean of all its takes and gets cleaner with each hearing. A record heard once fades in time; one heard often lasts.

Speaking. To say a word, the parrot reads the same links backwards, from the word to the record linked to it, and the voice plays that record’s sound.

Answering: settling

An exchange. The brain lives in moments of 300 ms. While you ask, it hears one word a moment, each as it is spoken, with its voice held. Its turn is the three quiet moments after your last word.

Settling. In each moment every neuron adds up its inputs and moves its activity toward that sum. Each move changes its neighbours’ inputs, so the whole brain shifts together, sweep after sweep, until every neuron agrees with its inputs. That settled state is the brain’s reading of the moment. At its turn, the voice neuron that settles highest says its word.

Its parts. Its eyes report the thing in focus and the things round it (1,528 inputs); its ear receives the word it hears (64 inputs). Four processing regions of 128 neurons each sit between them and an association region of 128 neurons, which also hears its own trace of the last moment and the words heard before. The voice has one neuron for each of its 47 words and one for silence. In all, 5,594 neurons and 1,182,336 synapses (pack/trained/brain.npz).

A thing is a pattern

There is no apple neuron. With the apple in focus, every region settles into a pattern of activity, and that whole pattern is the brain’s apple at that moment. Things that share a property share part of their patterns: the apple and the ball are both round.

A linear probe shows what a pattern holds: one weighted sum over the association region’s neurons, fitted on some rooms and tested on rooms the brain never saw. On this brain, with nothing heard, the probes read the thing in focus: its kind .99 (chance .13), colour .99 (.20), round 1.00 (.70), material .93 (.33), edible .99 (.67) and direction .96 (.27) (pack/trained/version.json).

The pistachio

A pistachio is the parrot’s dopamine: the signal that what it just did paid.

The brain’s lesson. The brain keeps each moment it spoke for a short while. When a pistachio comes, each kept moment becomes a lesson whose label is the word it said: nothing tells it the right answer. A lesson is two more settles, with the voice pulled a little toward its word and a little away from it. Each synapse compares its two neurons’ activity in the two settles and grows where they were more active together under the pull toward the word. No error is sent back through the network; each synapse reads only its own two neurons. Where the brain would give the paid answer by itself, it takes no lesson, so one pistachio leaves its other answers as they were.

The links’ lesson. The same pistachio strengthens the link from the sound it heard to the meaning it took it for, and from each word it said to the sound it said it with. A guess that no pistachio follows is doubted for a while.

Born and learned

Born: how the ear cuts and cleans a sound, how a record is written, told again and forgotten, how the records vote, how a brain settles, and how a lesson changes a synapse.

Learned: every sound it keeps, every link from a sound to a meaning, and every synapse of the trained brain. The trained brain learned its answers ahead of time, in a night of lessons on simulated rooms: names, then colour, then where things are, then every question mixed. In the page it learns from your pistachios.

Limits

  • The link between a sound and its meaning lives in the hearing layer, beside the trained brain. The brain receives each word as a code its training drew for that word.
  • It answers about one thing at a time, the thing in focus.
  • It does not take a free sentence apart: it matches a whole utterance to the sounds it keeps.
  • The trained brain sees each thing as its training world described it, from a table of kinds, colours and materials, and not through the drawn eyes.
  • It gets some questions wrong, among them the material of some things and the colour of the green ball; the page leaves those off its list.
