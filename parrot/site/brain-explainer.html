How the parrot’s brain works

The brain that answers your questions on this page was trained overnight in the meaning experiment (cadence#334). This page shows how it holds a thing, how it answers, what it remembers, how a word comes back to it, how a pistachio teaches it, and what it cannot do. Every claim comes from its code or from a measured reading, and every number says what it was measured on.

Which brainThe brain trained tonight runs beside the page’s composed body (its eyes, head and map of the table) and the page’s hearing (the sounds you teach it). Both trained brains on the page have one build: four processing regions and the association region, 3,090 neurons and 526,976 synapses. Readings of the brain are of the N2 colour brain (founder 33400110) unless a line names another; readings from the night’s development say so.

The brain at a glance

The brain is 3,090 neurons joined by 526,976 synapses, laid out the way a bird’s forebrain is. Point at a part of the drawing, or tap it, to light its routes.

  • Eyes (504 inputs). The thing in focus, seen again in a place of its own (the glimpse), then up to five things on the table. Each thing arrives as tuned readings: hue, lightness, six measures of shape, size, four of surface (gloss, roughness, translucency, softness), its direction from the head and its distance. No input says “apple” or “red”.
  • Ear (64 inputs). Each word arrives as a code of its own, 5 of the 64 inputs lit, drawn at random for this brain: a stand-in for the code of the word’s sound that Field L and NCM make in a bird.
  • Four processing regions of 128 neurons each, stacked. Each talks to the region below and the region above.
  • The association region (128 neurons; NCL in a bird) on top. It hears the eyes, the ear, the region below, its own pattern of the moment before (the working trace) and what was seen and heard in earlier moments (the heard history).
  • The voice (the song system): one neuron for each of 47 words, one for silence, and two for the beak (bite or not).
  • Beside the brain: the word records (words kept under patterns of the association), the page’s sound memory (the sounds you teach it) and the dopamine of a pistachio (the VTA).

A concept is a pattern

There is no apple neuron. With the apple in focus, every region settles into a pattern of activity over its neurons, and that whole pattern is the brain’s apple at that moment. Another apple, green and further away, gives a pattern close enough that the same reading finds “apple” in it; a ball gives a different one.

Things that share a property share part of the pattern. Apples and balls are round, and their patterns overlap on the neurons that carry round; apples and bananas are food, and theirs overlap where food is carried. In the night’s first brain (founder 33400106), the patterns nearest the apple’s were the lemon’s, the pistachio’s and the ball’s, and a lemon, a kind it never saw in training, read as round (.91) and edible (1.00).

How can anyone tell what a pattern holds? With a linear probe: one weighted sum over the neurons, fitted on some scenes and tested on scenes the brain never met. Where the sum reads a property well above chance, the pattern carries it. The table reads every property from one settled state (a thing in focus, nothing heard), region by region.

In its mind. The card of that name on the page uses probes of this kind on the association region, at each of the brain’s moments, for the thing in focus. It shows what the state holds and never fills a property from what the page knows of the room. Its held-out scores on this brain: kind .93 (chance .14), colour .86 (.52), round 1.00 (.68), material .81 (.36), edible .89 (.64), direction .40 (.29), distance .54 (.58). On the N1 names brain (founder 33400111) kind reads 1.00 and colour .14 against .35: the brain taught colour holds colour in its state, and the brain taught names alone does not.

How it answers: settling

The brain works in moments of 300 ms. In each moment it takes in what it sees and hears, and every neuron adds up its inputs and moves its activity toward that sum. Each move changes its neighbours’ inputs, so the whole brain shifts together, sweep after sweep, until every neuron’s activity agrees with its inputs: an equilibrium. That settled state is the brain’s reading of the moment. The voice neuron that settles highest says its word, or silence wins.

On the page’s brain a moment takes 32 to 64 sweeps, and the settle stops once every neuron’s equation holds within .003 (measured on “what is this?” with the apple in focus; it said “apple”).

From below and from above. A neuron of a processing region hears the region below on its basal dendrites: what is seen and heard. It hears the region above on a compartment of its own, the apical tuft: the question, and what the association makes of it, top-down. The top-down drive keeps its direction, and its size is held under half of the bottom-up drive (apical .5), so what the regions above expect can never drown what the parrot sees.

An exchange on the page. You type a question in Ask it. The brain hears it one word a moment, its voice held while you speak (a born inhibition, as a songbird’s HVC holds a call back while it listens). Its turn is the three silent moments after your last word. By then your words are out of the ear, and they reach the association through the working trace and the heard history. For a question about the whole room (“what objects are round?”) its focus visits each thing in turn, one a moment, and it may say a name at each.

Memory: synapses and records

The parrot keeps what it learns in two ways.

Synapses are slow and lasting. Every lesson moves many of the 526,976 synapses a little, and no single synapse holds a fact. What the changes keep is a way of settling: which pattern a thing evokes, and which word the voice says for which pattern.

Records are fast: one item, written in one go and read back whole. Two stores of records serve the parrot.

The word records (in the trained brain’s files; 2,048 on the page). When the trainer gives a word as an answer or as a correction, the word is kept under the association’s pattern at the moment the question ended: the pattern is the key, the word is the value. At the parrot’s turn its pattern looks the key up. Where the three nearest records hold the same word and the nearest lies close (cosine .85 or more), that word is played back into its ear; where they disagree, nothing comes back. In training a record read at a turn is rewritten under the present pattern once its reply is judged (reconsolidation: a recalled memory is open to change).

The night needed them: in development, saying a heard word back across the silent gap read .03 to .14 after 300 blocks without a memory of the word, and .94 to .97 with the records. Three must agree because at a question’s end the association’s patterns lie very close together (cosine .987 on average in the night’s development), so the single nearest record named things at chance (.14). With the rule, an unclear cue recalls nothing.

The sound memory (the page’s hearing, beside the brain) keeps the sounds you teach it, as sound:

  1. Cut out. The page listens for a sound that stands above the room, from where it starts to a quarter second of quiet. It keeps voice-like sounds only (a click under 120 ms, a fan’s hiss and a steady hum are left out) and takes the room’s background out of them.
  2. A word it knows? It lines the sound up with the nearest words it keeps, sound for sound in time, so a take said faster or slower lines up. Where the two lie close, it is a word it knows.
  3. Deeper with each hearing. A known word heard again deepens its record (depth 1, 3, 5 and on to 19 after ten hearings), and its kept sound is mixed with the new take, the new take weighing one part in n. A record of depth 2.5 or more is one it can say back; a word heard once fades.
  4. Linked to the thing in focus. Synapses link the sound to the kind of thing the parrot looked at while it heard it (one unit per kind), stronger with each hearing, and stronger again with a pistachio after the parrot says it.
  5. Recalled by the thing. With a thing in focus the links are read backwards: of the records deep enough to say, the one most strongly linked to the apple is the apple’s word, and its kept sound is what the voice plays.

Measured on the page’s hearing (the demo’s evidence files): one word said ten times made 1 record of depth 18.95, linked to the apple at .40 and to every other thing at 0. With varied takes, the kept sound’s distance to the clean word fell from .072 after one hearing to .029 after sixteen. Thirty seconds of hiss, clicks and hum made no record.

Recall: how a word comes back

Ask “what is this?” with the apple in focus, and follow the word:

  1. Focus. The apple is seen in the glimpse, in a place of its own, beside the whole scene.
  2. The state. All regions settle on the apple’s pattern, with the question’s words held in the working trace and the heard history.
  3. The word records. As the turn opens, the pattern at the question’s end looks up the records. Where the three nearest agree, their word is played into the ear, which reaches the voice directly and through the association.
  4. The voice. The voice neuron that settles highest says its word. The synapses from the association to the voice learned which pattern calls for which word.
  5. The sound. The page says the word in the sound you taught it for the apple, found through the sound memory’s link to the apple. With no such link the word shows as text.

How a question selects what to read. The state holds a thing’s properties together whatever is asked: in the association, probes read red at 1.00 and round at .98 to 1.00 under “what is this” and under “what colour has the apple” alike. What changes is the answer: the same brain names the thing (.97) or says its colour (.66). On these readings the selection happens at the readout. The question’s words, held in the trace and the history, join the thing’s pattern in the association, and the synapses to the voice learned which word that combination calls for. (The meaning lane’s readings for the top-down review, on this brain.)

How things link together

  • Thing to thing: by the shared parts of their patterns (round, food). Nearest to the apple: the lemon, the pistachio, the ball (founder 33400106).
  • Seen thing to word: the synapses from the association to the voice. This route is learned, and it runs one way, from seeing to saying.
  • Heard word to the same word said: the ear’s direct route to the voice. It says back a word it hears (.94 on the N2 colour brain, 1.00 on the N1 names brain).
  • Heard word to its thing: weak. A word heard alone does not call up its thing’s pattern (the last section shows the test).
  • Question to answer word: the word records, keyed by the pattern at the question’s end.
  • Word to sound: the page’s sound memory, where your sound is linked by synapses to a kind of thing.

Rewards: the pistachio

A pistachio is the parrot’s dopamine: the signal from the midbrain (the VTA) that what it just did paid.

The lesson a pistachio gates. The parrot’s reply is kept for a short while: each moment it spoke, what it saw and heard then, and the word it said. If a pistachio comes, each kept moment becomes a lesson whose label is the parrot’s own word. Nothing tells it the right answer; the pistachio makes lasting what it did. With no pistachio the kept moments are dropped.

A lesson in two settles. First the brain settles freely, as it did when it replied. Then it settles again with its voice gently pulled toward the label, and once more pulled away from it (±β). Every synapse compares the activity of its two neurons in the two pulled settles: it grows where they were more active together under the pull toward the label, and shrinks where less. No error is sent backward through the network; each synapse reads only its own two neurons.

The pistachio comes after the act. In training a right reply earns the trainer’s click at once (a sound the voice cannot make) and a pistachio in the beak two moments later. The lesson is taken when the pay is decided, on a copy of the brain as it was at each moment it spoke, and its change is added to the living brain. That kept copy is the act’s mark, its eligibility: in a bird, dopamine turns such a mark into a lasting change when it arrives 0.3 to 2 s after the act, and three moments are 0.9 s. On the page the pistachio button takes the lessons at once, on the kept moments.

The actor-critic. The brain also carries the classic reward learner. Every synapse keeps a fading trace of its part in each act (it fades to .93 a moment, so about .80 is left when the nut is in the beak), a critic predicts the pay, and the surprise (pay less prediction) times each trace changes the synapse. In this brain its step is about a thousandth of a lesson’s. That matters: on its own it taught names, colours and places only .00 to .14 in 3,000 to 4,000 paid trials of each (chance .125; the night’s first development run), while a lesson on the parrot’s own act taught it to say heard words back at 1.00. Turned up 100 and 300 times on this brain, it made “what is red?” flicker to .13 to .16 and back to 0, and names fell. On the page the brain answers with its best word, which keeps no trace, so there the pistachio’s whole effect is the lesson.

One pistachio is strong. On the page, one pistachio for “apple” (three lessons, one for each moment it said it) turned its answer to “what colour has the apple?” from “red” to “apple” (the parrot-demo lane’s reading on the N2 colour brain).

Corrections and sleep

A correction. After a wrong reply the trainer says the right word, the parrot’s voice held. The word is kept as a word record under the question’s pattern. In training it is also a lesson, on a copy of the brain as it was when it replied, whose label is the voice’s own neuron for the word it heard: an auditory-vocal mirror, as songbird HVC neurons fire alike when the bird sings a syllable and when it hears it. Learning on the states the learner itself reached, labelled by a teacher, is the DAgger idea. In development the parrot’s own echo as the label was the right word only 11 to 24% of the time and taught noise; the mirror’s label repaired that. On this page a correction keeps the record and the parrot hears the word; the page takes no correction lesson.

Rehearsal. In training, three blocks in ten revisit the questions of earlier steps, so the lessons of a new step do not wash out the old words. Without it the online control learners lost each step’s words at the next step.

Sleep. Between training steps every synapse’s efficacy is halved. Lessons grow efficacies, and large efficacies drive neurons up to their ceiling. A neuron at its ceiling barely moves when the voice is pulled and passes little of the pull on, so around it the two settles of a lesson look alike and few synapses change. Halving keeps each neuron’s ranking of its inputs, so what the brain knows survives, and it brings the neurons back to where a pull moves them.

Measured in the night’s development: imitation’s lessons grew the efficacies (mean .05 to .13, largest .18 to 3.18) and put 19% of the association’s neurons above .98. That brain, taught names with the right word as the label, reached .27 in 400 blocks; halved first, 1.00 in 100 (a fresh brain: 80). After halving it said heard words back at .99.

Outside the state. The design asks that what a thing means lives in the settled state, or in records keyed by it. These live elsewhere: a word’s sound (the voice has one neuron per word, and the sound comes from the page’s sound memory); a heard word’s form (a fixed code drawn per word, standing in for its sound); the correction’s label (the voice’s neuron for the heard word, matched by identity); the world’s prototypes (a kind’s shape, a colour’s hue, a material’s surface, from the page’s spec); and the written word, which is nowhere.

What it cannot do yet

  • A heard word does not call up its thing. Heard alone in an empty room, every kind’s word settles the association on one state, which the probes read as “ball, yellow, cloth”. The kind reads right for one word of nine, the ball. Seeing leads to naming; hearing does not lead to the thing.
  • “What is red?” reads .00. It says the colour of a named thing (.83 on kind-colour pairs it never saw) and cannot find the thing of a colour; scanning the room, it names every thing it looks at. A multiplicative top-down gate, tried as an arm, lifted it to .16 at most.
  • Places are weak. Direction reads .48 against .30 and distance .59 against .54 in the association; this brain was never taught places or relations.
  • The written word is outside the state. It never sees letters.
  • The sound lives beside the brain. The brain picks a word neuron, and the sound comes from the page’s sound memory. Looking at a thing with nothing heard, its word records give the thing’s own name 4% of the time (a word at all, 52%).
  • Lessons on the page can overwrite. One pistachio for a name turned a right colour answer into the name.

What is being tried. Binding hearing and seeing by a pull inside the brain’s own learning rule: the word heard alone is pulled toward the state that the word and the sight make together, and the glimpse alone toward the same state, with the lesson’s settles and its normalized step. On the N1 names brain (founder 33400111) the word alone then read its kind at .56 after 50 rounds and fell back to .22 by 75, and saying back fell to .53 to .63. A plain contrast between two settles did not bind at all. The open question is whether binding needs synapses of its own. A second arm keeps a name’s record under a moment of just looking at the thing: the records then give the thing’s own name 48% of the time, against 3.9% without.
